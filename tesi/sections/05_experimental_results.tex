\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:experiments}

This section presents the empirical results of the five experiments introduced
in Chapter~\ref{sec:experimentdesign}. Section~\ref{subsec:res01} compares the
baseline models, and Section~\ref{subsec:res02} analyses per-agent parameter
sensitivity. Section~\ref{subsec:res03} examines the effect of the output
token budget, while Section~\ref{subsec:res04} studies inference-time compute
expansion through Chain-of-Thought refinement and also probes the effectiveness
of Best-of-$N$ sampling on the lookup step. Finally,
Section~\ref{subsec:res05} reports the final Pareto confirmation, including
model-level summaries, quality-energy frontiers, and prompt-difficulty analyses
over the evaluated candidate configurations.

\subsection{Test 01 - Baseline Model Comparison}
\label{subsec:res01}

Test~01 establishes the quality and efficiency baseline for the three
candidate models before any parameter tuning is applied. The experimental
design is described in Section~\ref{subsec:test01}: each model runs with its
Ollama-recommended sampling defaults over all 20 benchmark prompts, with no
agent step varied. Three repeats per model were collected, yielding
3~models $\times$ 3~repeats $\times$ 20~prompts $= 180$ benchmark rows.

\subsubsection{Quality Metrics and End-to-End Interpretation}

The benchmark runner reports a standard \texttt{quality\_mean} defined as the
arithmetic mean of the three component score means:
\begin{equation}
\texttt{quality\_mean} = \frac{1}{3}\bigl(\overline{\texttt{csv\_iou}} +
                                           \overline{\texttt{text\_score}} +
                                           \overline{\texttt{vis\_score}}\bigr)
\label{eq:quality_mean}
\end{equation}


\textbf{XXX ridire che le metriche base sono gia' tra 0 e 1 altrimenti la formula usata non va bene XXX}
where the averages are taken over the subset of prompts that produced a
scorable output for each component. This definition is consistent across
all tests and is used for continuity, but it can mask pipeline failures:
when the lookup step returns an empty or erroneous SQL result, the text
and visualization agents are never reached, so the resulting missing
scores are simply excluded from the denominators of the text and
visualization means, leaving the official \texttt{quality\_mean}
artificially high.

To address this, two additional quality views are used throughout this test:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Component E2E quality} — the same three-component mean,
          but any expected score absent due to an upstream pipeline failure
          is counted as~$0.0$ rather than excluded. This is the primary
          accuracy metric for comparisons in this section.
    \item \textbf{Prompt E2E quality} — the per-prompt mean of the expected
          component scores, with missing expected outputs counted as~$0.0$.
          This view penalises each failed prompt as a single unit,
          regardless of how many components were expected from it.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Per-Model Error Analysis}
\label{subsubsec:test01errors}

\paragraph{\texttt{gemma4:e4b}.}
The main failures occur on prompts~17 and~4, both requiring a comparison of
average monthly revenue across different store segmentation dimensions over
2022 and~2023. The generated SQL queries attempt the correct nested monthly
aggregation logic but reference incorrect column aliases or date fields in the
outer subquery, causing the lookup step to return empty results. A further
partial failure occurs on prompt~9 (store cities exceeding a revenue threshold
as a bar chart), where text and visualization outputs are produced but with
reduced scores across repeats. These failure types motivate the lookup-step
targeted interventions evaluated in Test~04.

\paragraph{\texttt{gemma4:26b}.}
The model does not fail on SQL. Its main weakness is concentrated in the
visualization agent on two prompts:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prompt~5} (promo vs.\ non-promo revenue as a bar chart):
          the chart type and data are approximately correct but the
          visualization configuration does not match the expected grouping
          structure (\texttt{mean quality}~$= 0.389$, 2~missing slots).
    \item \textbf{Prompt~11} (monthly revenue split by promo flag as a
          grouped bar chart): similar grouping failure
          (\texttt{mean quality}~$= 0.500$, 2~missing slots).
\end{itemize}

These failures suggest that the larger Gemma model requires targeted
visualization tuning, as explored in Tests~02 and~03.

\paragraph{\texttt{mistral-\allowbreak small3.2:24b}.}
The model incurs the largest number of missing expected score slots (12),
all concentrated on difficulty-4 prompts~4 and~17 (6~slots each). Both
prompts require nested monthly revenue aggregation across store segmentation
dimensions; both score exactly 0.000 quality across all three repeats,
meaning the SQL failure recurs deterministically. Analysis completeness is
a secondary weakness: several difficulty-1 through~3 prompts receive partial
text scores due to incomplete analytical coverage or output schema mismatches.

\subsubsection{Summary}

The main findings of Test~01 can be summarised as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Mistral Small is the Pareto baseline.}
          \texttt{mistral-\allowbreak small3.2:24b} achieves the highest component E2E
          quality (0.827), the lowest latency (48.9~s/prompt), and the lowest
          energy (0.00601~kWh/prompt). It serves as the primary efficiency
          reference for all subsequent tests.

    \item \textbf{Gemma~E4B is close on aggregate quality but more expensive.}
          With three repeats, \texttt{gemma4:e4b} reaches 0.821 component E2E
          quality --- within 0.7\,\% of Mistral --- but at 1.47$\times$ the
          energy and 1.54$\times$ the latency. It remains a viable candidate
          when absolute quality is prioritised over efficiency.

    \item \textbf{Gemma~26B is held back by visualization.}
          \texttt{gemma4:26b} achieves the strongest CSV and text component
          scores, but its visualization E2E score (0.500) and aggregate
          quality (0.772) are the weakest overall. Without targeted
          visualization tuning it is Pareto-dominated.

    \item \textbf{Failure modes are step-specific.}
          Mistral and Gemma~E4B mainly lose quality through recurrent lookup
          failures on hard aggregation prompts, while Gemma~26B mainly loses
          quality in the visualization agent. This motivates the agent-specific
          parameter sweeps in the following tests.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Test 02 - Per-Agent Sensitivity Analysis (OFAT)}
\label{subsec:res02}

Test~02 applies a One-Factor-At-a-Time (OFAT) protocol to quantify how
individual static sampling hyperparameters affect each agent step in
isolation. The experimental design is described in
Section~\ref{subsec:test02}: each of the five parameters
(\texttt{temperature}, \texttt{top\_p}, \texttt{top\_k},
\texttt{repeat\_penalty}, \texttt{repeat\_last\_n}) is swept
individually across ten values per step, producing 153 configurations
per model. Each configuration is repeated twice over a 15-prompt subset
of the benchmark, reduced from the full 20-prompt set to limit total
run time while preserving the original difficulty distribution across
all four SQL complexity levels (difficulty~1: 3~prompts, difficulty~2:
4~prompts, difficulty~3: 5~prompts, difficulty~4: 3~prompts).

Table~\ref{tab:test02overall} reports the aggregate statistics averaged
across all 153 configurations and repeats. The gap from
Test~01 persists: \texttt{mistral-\allowbreak small3.2:24b} runs at 67.2~s and
0.00416~kWh per prompt while \texttt{gemma4:26b} requires 189.0~s and
0.01189~kWh. \texttt{gemma4:e4b} sits between them at 99.8~s and

\subsubsection{Per-Model Analysis}

\paragraph{\texttt{gemma4:e4b}.}

\texttt{gemma4:e4b} is the most sensitive model in Test~02. Its range is 0.223
at lookup, 0.196 at visualization, and 0.191 at analysis. The largest useful
signal is lookup tuning: \texttt{lookup\_top\_p=0.94} improves prompt E2E
quality by~$+0.117$ and \texttt{csv\_iou} by~$+0.139$ with only a small energy
increase. The negative side is just as important: analysis settings such as
\texttt{repeat\_last\_n=128} and low \texttt{top\_p} can degrade quality by
about~0.13. Thus E4B should be treated as a high-leverage but fragile small
thinking model, motivating the later focus on E4B lookup repair.

\paragraph{\texttt{gemma4:26b}.}

The sensitivity profile of \texttt{gemma4:26b} is strongly step-specific
and consistently asymmetric. The visualization step is unambiguously the
highest-leverage target: every parameter family improves it, and the
gains are large enough (up to $+6.8\%$ quality, $+5.2\%$
\texttt{vis\_score}) to be practically meaningful and to more than
offset the small-delta uncertainty identified by the matched-repeat check. The lookup step shows
meaningful positive potential ($+3.2\%$ quality via
\texttt{repeat\_last\_n\_96}) but also a very steep downside risk: a
single bad \texttt{top\_k} value can collapse quality by~$-12.3\%$,
likely by disrupting the structured SQL generation. The analysis step is
the most fragile: temperature changes in particular push the model into
degenerate outputs, and the safe operating window for this step is
narrow around the baseline.

\paragraph{\texttt{mistral-\allowbreak small3.2:24b}.}

\texttt{mistral-\allowbreak small3.2:24b} is almost entirely insensitive to the
parameters tested. The best achievable quality delta at any step is
$+0.007$, which is within the small-delta uncertainty range. The model's
low baseline temperature (0.15) and the non-thinking architecture
appear to make its output distribution far less responsive to sampling
parameter perturbations than the thinking model. The practical
implication is that static parameter tuning offers negligible quality
improvement for Mistral, and that pursuing efficiency gains for this
model requires structural changes rather than hyperparameter search.
The sole exception is the \texttt{analysis\_repeat\_penalty\_1.2}
misconfiguration described above, which serves as a warning against
assuming that insensitivity to improvement implies insensitivity to
regression.

\subsubsection{Summary}

Test~02 establishes that sensitivity to static sampling parameter tuning
is a model-level property, not a step-level one:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{gemma4:e4b} shows the widest sensitivity range of
          any model in the test.} Its lookup step alone spans 0.223 quality
          points between the best and worst static setting, exceeding any step
          of Gemma~26B or Mistral. Starting from the weakest lookup
          baseline~(0.806), \texttt{lookup\_top\_p\_0p94} delivers the
          strongest single-axis gain of the entire sweep at~$+0.117$ quality
          over baseline with low energy overhead.

    \item \textbf{\texttt{gemma4:26b} is highly tunable but fragile.}
          Sensitivity ranges reach~0.155 for lookup and~0.118 for analysis.
          The visualization step is the most rewarding target: all parameter
          families improve it, with the best configurations delivering up
          to~$+6.8\%$ quality at low energy overhead.

    \item \textbf{\texttt{mistral-\allowbreak small3.2:24b} is insensitive.}
          No parameter at any step moves quality by more than $+0.007$,
          confirming that this model's efficiency advantage from Test~01
          is robust to misconfiguration but also leaves little room for
          improvement through static hyperparameter tuning.

    \item \textbf{The best repeated Gemma~26B gains still target
          visualization.} E4B lookup dominates the top-gain table, but
          Gemma~26B's repeated evidence still points to visualization as its
          main tuning lever.

    \item \textbf{Temperature reduction hurts Gemma~26B analysis.}
          The thinking model's analysis quality degrades when temperature
          is lowered below the baseline of~1.0, suggesting that the
          model's thinking process benefits from sampling diversity.

    \item \textbf{Prompt difficulty magnifies tuning effects.} The largest
          gains and losses concentrate on hard prompts, especially for the
          thinking Gemma models.
\end{enumerate}

quality axis when the cap is raised, with a slight energy saving at
lower caps — consistent with the compression effect discussed earlier.

the ceiling available to the model. If the model finishes before hitting the
ceiling, energy remains almost unchanged. For this reason, when a
model-step pair reacts well to additional headroom, as Gemma~26B
visualization does, the rational choice is to allocate the larger cap. The
cost of allowing more tokens is small, while the cost of denying them can be
a hard truncation or malformed output. Conversely, the Gemma~E4B lookup and
visualization curves show that generous caps must still be bounded: beyond
the feasible window, additional headroom encourages longer but less reliable
outputs.

The lower quadrants are populated by configurations where a cap that
is too restrictive causes truncated or structurally broken outputs,
particularly for \texttt{gemma4:e4b} at the visualization step.
These are the only points where energy and quality move together

Figures~\ref{fig:test03_diff_sensitivity} and~\ref{fig:test03_diff_delta} examine
whether the effect of \texttt{max\_tokens} varies with prompt difficulty, from two
complementary angles. Figure~\ref{fig:test03_diff_sensitivity} reports the
\emph{sensitivity range} --- the gap between the best and worst quality across all
token caps in each (model, step, difficulty) slice --- which measures how much the cap
choice matters at all, in either direction. Figure~\ref{fig:test03_diff_delta} instead
reports the \emph{best-cap delta} --- the quality gain of the best cap over the baseline
cap (the best cap is annotated in each cell) --- which measures how much can be gained by
re-tuning the cap relative to its default. The two views can diverge: a slice may show a
large sensitivity range yet a near-zero best-cap delta when the baseline cap is already
optimal but an overly tight cap would sharply degrade quality.

The token cap behaves as a failure-prevention bound rather than a smooth quality
dial, and its impact is strongest on slices where the default cap causes
truncation or malformed outputs. The most striking case is \texttt{gemma4:e4b}
visualization at difficulty~4: reducing the cap from the 7000-token baseline to
4000 raises quality by~$+0.355$, because the model generates overly long or
structurally incorrect chart code at the default budget. The two heatmap views can
also diverge: at \texttt{gemma4:e4b} lookup difficulty~1 the default 5000-token cap is
already optimal, so no larger cap improves quality (best-cap delta of~$+0.000$, quality
staying at~0.939), yet the slice is far from insensitive --- dropping the cap to 3000
collapses quality (a sensitivity range of~0.342). These results support using
generous but not blindly maximal caps per agent: a cap that is too tight causes
discrete failures on complex prompts, while an excessively high cap rarely helps
and can encourage verbose outputs that reduce quality.

\subsubsection{Summary}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{max\_tokens} is a per-agent safety bound,
          not a universal accuracy knob.} For the majority of
          (model, step) pairs the optimal cap is either the baseline or
          one step away, and quality changes are small. The exceptions
          are structural: where low caps cause truncation-induced
          failures, raising the budget has a large discrete effect.

    \item \textbf{Gemma~26B visualization is genuinely token-constrained.}
          Raising the cap from 7000 to 10000 yields a $+0.054$
          \texttt{vis\_score} gain with negligible energy change. This
          is the clearest actionable finding of Test~03. Since no plateau
          is visible at the top of the tested ladder, future runs could
          probe a slightly larger visualization cap for this model.

    \item \textbf{Gemma~26B analysis benefits from a tighter cap.}
          The 4000-token cap outperforms the 7000-token baseline by
          $+0.020$ quality. The compression forces the model to
          produce a more concise, less diffuse analysis response.

    \item \textbf{Gemma~E4B is non-monotone at both lookup and visualization.}
          Higher caps beyond 5000 (lookup) and 7000 (visualization)
          cause quality regressions, with the 10000-token visualization
          cap producing the worst \texttt{vis\_score} ($0.51$) of any
          configuration in the test. This model should therefore use
          bounded, agent-specific caps rather than blindly generous ones.

    \item \textbf{Mistral is insensitive to \texttt{max\_tokens}.}
          The quality range does not exceed~0.025 at any step, and the
          baseline cap is optimal or tied-optimal at all three steps.
          Mistral can therefore be run with moderate caps without sacrificing
          accuracy.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Test 04 - Compute Expansion via Chain-of-Thought}
\label{subsec:res04}

Test~04 evaluates Chain-of-Thought (CoT) iterations as a compute
expansion strategy for the lookup and visualization steps. Each
configuration requests \texttt{cot\_n}~$\in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ refinement
iterations, where \texttt{cot\_n}~$= 1$ is the single-call baseline. An
early-stop mechanism halts the loop when the model signals that the
previous output is already satisfactory, so the executed depth can be
lower than the requested depth. The test was run on
\texttt{gemma4:e4b} and \texttt{mistral-\allowbreak small3.2:24b} only
(3~repeats $\times$ 8~configs $\times$ 10~prompts = 240~rows each).
\texttt{gemma4:26b} was excluded because its main weakness (visualization)
was already addressed by the token-budget increase in Test~03, its SQL
and text steps were near saturation, and its per-prompt cost is already
the highest of the three models, making additional compute iterations
particularly expensive relative to the expected gain.

\subsubsection{Summary}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Lookup CoT is the most effective single intervention
          found for \texttt{gemma4:e4b}.} \texttt{cot\_n=2} raises
          quality by~$+18.6$ points and \texttt{csv\_iou} by~$+19.7$
          points at a cost multiplier of~$1.38\times$. This directly
          addresses the SQL-generation brittleness identified in Test~01.

    \item \textbf{Lookup CoT also benefits Mistral, modestly.}
          \texttt{cot\_n=3} yields the best Mistral result ($+6.3$
          quality points, $+9.0$ \texttt{csv\_iou} points), though the
          gain is three times smaller than for Gemma~E4B.

    \item \textbf{Visualization CoT is harmful for both models.}
          Every depth above~1 degrades \texttt{vis\_score}, with
          Gemma~E4B \texttt{cot\_n=3} causing a $-16.1$ point direct
          metric regression. Visualization CoT should not be used.

    \item \textbf{The effective CoT depth saturates near~2.} The
          early-stop mechanism makes \texttt{cot\_n=3} and
          \texttt{cot\_n=4} behave similarly to \texttt{cot\_n=2} for
          the lookup step. Requesting depth~2 is therefore the most
          cost-efficient default for both models. For Gemma~E4B,
          \texttt{cot\_n=3} is useful as a hard-prompt repair setting
          because it fixes the difficulty-4 lookup slice, but
          \texttt{cot\_n=4} should not be the default: it rarely reaches
          the fourth iteration and its extra aggregate gain is small
          relative to the additional cost and variance. For
          \texttt{mistral-\allowbreak small3.2:24b}, \texttt{cot\_n=3} is the nominal
          quality peak ($+0.063$), but the gain over depth~2 is much
          smaller than the Gemma gain and most high-depth runs stop early.
          Depth~3 should therefore be reserved for maximum lookup quality,
          not for the energy-aware default.

    \item \textbf{Lookup Best-of-N helps but is dominated by CoT (Test~04b).}
          On the \texttt{gemma4:e4b} lookup step, Best-of-3 candidate diversity
          does improve quality when the temperature range is well chosen --- the
          medium range $[0.6,1.0]$ reaches $+0.054$ quality and the strongest BoN
          \texttt{csv\_iou} ($0.867$), while the narrow range is harmful --- but
          the local no-GT selector exploits this diversity only weakly, still
          picking the first candidate in $86.7$--$96.7\,\%$ of runs. A single
          static \texttt{lookup\_temp\_0.8} is the best low-cost point ($+0.053$
          quality below baseline energy), and \texttt{lookup\_cot\_n2} dominates
          the best BoN range on quality, CSV similarity, completion, and energy.
          Best-of-N is therefore a useful diagnostic for candidate diversity, but
          CoT with SQL-execution feedback remains the more effective
          compute-expansion strategy for lookup.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Summary}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Test~05 is the final confirmation stage.}
          It compares the strongest end-to-end policies under identical
          execution conditions to determine the final quality--energy
          recommendation.

    \item \textbf{The final Pareto front is not a pure model-size ranking.}
          The larger Gemma~26B MoE improves with visualization-focused tuning
          but remains dominated in the evaluated quality-energy frontier.

    \item \textbf{Mistral defines the evaluated frontier.}
          Mistral configurations occupy both the low-energy region and the
          highest-quality endpoint once lookup CoT is added.

    \item \textbf{Gemma~E4B remains a close high-quality alternative.}
          The final E4B candidate combines lookup CoT, static lookup
          temperature, and lower downstream token caps, reaching quality only
          slightly below the best Mistral endpoint. Full completion is in fact
          one axis where E4B stays more consistent than several Mistral
          candidates, which leave a small fraction of expected outputs
          incomplete.

    \item \textbf{Prompt difficulty determines which Pareto point is best.}
          Mistral is the natural choice for the evaluated easy, medium, and
          difficulty-4 prompts; Gemma~E4B remains the best evaluated endpoint
          on difficulty~3, and broader hard-prompt testing is needed before
          generalising this ordering to more complex workloads.
\end{enumerate}
