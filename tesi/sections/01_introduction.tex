\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
The growing adoption of Large Language Models (LLMs) in enterprise software has opened
a concrete path toward natural language interfaces for structured data. Systems capable of
translating a business question into a SQL query, executing it, and returning both a textual
analysis and a chart represent a significant step forward in making data-driven decision making
accessible to non-technical users. At the same time, deploying such systems in production
raises practical questions that go beyond accuracy: how much time does each inference take,
how does it scale with configuration changes, and what is the energy cost of running LLM-backed
pipelines on real hardware?

This thesis addresses both aspects. On the application side, it presents the design and
implementation of a \textit{Sales Agent} --- an LLM-powered conversational system that enables
business analysts to query a synthetic retail dataset using plain English. Given a natural
language question, the agent autonomously selects the appropriate processing steps, generates
and executes a SQL query against Parquet files via DuckDB, produces a textual interpretation
of the results, and optionally renders a Matplotlib chart. The agent is orchestrated through
LangGraph~\cite{langgraph}, a framework for stateful graph-based LLM pipelines, and supports
two interchangeable backends: locally-hosted open-weight models via Ollama and cloud-based
models via the OpenAI API. A hierarchical YAML configuration system exposes the inference
parameters studied in the experiments --- sampling temperature, top-$p$, top-$k$, best-of-$N$ candidates, and
Chain-of-Thought refinement iterations --- making every run fully reproducible and configurable
without code changes.

On the experimental side, the primary focus of the thesis is the systematic profiling of the
agent under varying configurations. A benchmark of 20 analytical questions, stratified across
four SQL difficulty levels, is used to evaluate output quality across three complementary
dimensions: a graded ground-truth table-similarity score for data retrieval
accuracy, an LLM-as-judge score for the natural language analysis, and a
further LLM-based score for visualisation correctness. A central motivation is
to test whether larger thinking models are always the best choice, or whether
smaller thinking models and larger non-thinking models can be more efficient
once the agent steps are tuned. The evaluation is structured as a sequence of five step-isolated
experiments. The first establishes a quality and energy baseline for three locally-hosted
open-weight models --- Gemma~4~E4B, Gemma~4~26B, and Mistral~Small~3.2~24B. The subsequent
tests vary one agent step at a time: a one-factor-at-a-time sensitivity analysis across
sampling hyperparameters, a token-budget ladder, and a compute-expansion study combining
best-of-$N$ sampling with Chain-of-Thought refinement. A final Pareto confirmation run then
identifies the configurations that best balance output quality against inference cost. For
every run, execution time and energy consumption --- tracked per step using
CodeCarbon~\cite{lottick2019codecarbon} --- are recorded alongside quality scores, providing
actionable guidance for deploying LLM agents under latency or sustainability constraints.

\textcolor{red}{This thesis addresses the following research questions:

RQ1. How do the evaluated locally hosted LLMs compare in terms of end-to-end output quality, execution latency, and energy consumption when used as the backbone of a multi-step data-analysis agent?

RQ2. How sensitive are the individual agent steps—data lookup, textual analysis, and visualisation—to changes in sampling parameters and output-token budgets?

RQ3. Under which conditions does additional inference-time computation, through iterative refinement or Best-of-N sampling, improve output quality sufficiently to justify its additional latency and energy cost?

RQ4. How does SQL-query difficulty influence the relative performance of the evaluated models and the resulting quality–energy trade-off?

RQ5. Which of the evaluated model and inference-setting combinations provide the most favourable empirical trade-offs between strict end-to-end quality, completion, latency, and local energy consumption? 

Accordingly, the main contributions of this thesis are: (i) a configurable multi-step data agent; (ii) a per-step quality, latency, and energy profiling pipeline; (iii) a controlled sensitivity analysis across model-step pairs; and (iv) empirical deployment recommendations based on quality–energy trade-offs.}
